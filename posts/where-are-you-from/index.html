Where are you from?

March 3, 2026 kurdistan

The most common type of question a stranger gets is about where they are from. As a foreigner trying to socialize and integrate into a new society, this question becomes part of your daily communication.

I imagine for most, it's trivial question to answer. Just name the country of your origin, or if you're a local you'd say the name of your city, town or village.

I also imagine that sometimes a person is either shy or even hesitant to name their place of origin, in the fear of being judged or stereotyped. We don't choose where we are born, yet it defines us.

One wonders why it's so natural and expected for us to ask about the origins of a complete stranger on our first contact. Maybe it's the consequence of living in separate communities, and that we are only protecting the inner group from potentially dangerous outsiders; seldom it has been the fate of humanity to be divided.

The quick judgments some people make based on this answer are to me tragic and maybe sometimes also humorous. At this day and age, It is a sad sight to see when an adult judges a person in their entirety based on the place of their origin.

Since I moved to Europe, naturally I have been asked this question a lot, and it has been always tricky to answer. For reasons beyond my control, the expected answer, which is "from Iraq." is neither really true nor satisfactory, as the truth is far more complicated.

Here I write about the answer I rather give.

The short answer: From Kurdistan.

The long answer is that I was born inside the national borders of a country called Iraq, located in West Asia[1]. A country that is foreign to me, and also a country which sees me as a foreigner; an anomaly which shouldn't exist.

I am born of Kurdish parents, who were born inside the national borders of Iran. In their youth they both joined the noble struggle for their democratic rights as Kurdish people inside Iran. When they got married, they had to flee to the mountains at the border between Iraq and Iran, and after many hardships and sacrifices, they had to flee once again, and eventually settle in Iraq in small communities of people whom they shared the same struggle with; I was born in one such community.

Iraq is the country where I was born at, but that I don't have a sense belonging to. A country that has a problem with my identity, a nuisance that it's better without, and country that until recently it would go out of its way to eliminate my kind in futile, but brutal attempts of assimilation in hopes that we would be erased. A country where its flag and pride reminds me of the slaughter of my people.

I belong to the Kurds. I belong to the mountains, valleys of Zagros. I belong to the countless proud nomadic tribes of Kurds which roamed and settled these mountains for millennia. I belong to the hardy people of the mountains. I belong to the people of "Mem û Zîn"[2], the lovers. I am a follower of Hêmin[3], the poet. I belong to the freedom fighters against injustice and fascism. I belong to Kurdistan, the land of the Kurds.

Kurds are a proud people, not claiming to be anything else than being themselves. In contrast, of modern uniformity and brutal pressure for assimilation, they have proudly kept their cultural identity and traditions. Kurds speak, sing, and love in their language, they wear their traditional clothes lovingly, and they dance hand in hand, sharing their joy in rhythm.

I claim that in every proud Kurd there is a deep core authenticity. May it be because of their heritage as hardy people, their environment, or various other factors. The fact of the matter is, from every Kurd you can expect a form of brotherly love, kindness, and honor. If that is not observed, they have tragically lost their ways.

The mountains of Kurdistan, those lush mountains of thousand springs, have been the generous host of many tribes of people including the Kurds. Those mountains that to this day are being defiled by modern nation state that failed to resolve their domestic problems and degrade to barbarism, as so it's the way of nation states.

A people with their own distinct identity, who are on their lands, in the eyes of nation states, the land does not rightfully belong to them and are not allowed to govern themselves, and are considered as a problem, stubborn people who just won't assimilate; an anomaly, perhaps.

The failure of nation states in West Asia is most apparent when it comes to the question of the Kurds[4]. The Kurds, with a population of roughly 20-30 million people in total, are currently the largest group of people without a recognized homeland. Kurds inhabit the lands which are currently divided among Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria. The modern history of oppression against the Kurdish people is tragically long, as the existence of Kurds, and other minorities, is seen as a threat to the creation myth of such nations; the myth that they are all one people with one identity inside some arbitrary boundary of land. Anything that goes against this narrative is a threat. And naturally Kurds, along with other minorities are seen as a national threat to the ideal and fetishized unity of nationalists, and the perfect common enemy of dictators.

As Ibrahim Ahmad[5] says in his poem

I am neither Arab nor Iranian,
Nor am I a mountain Turk.
My very history itself declares
That I am Kurdish, and from Kurdistan.

I dream of a day that I will see my homeland free, and West Asia free from its hard colonial nationalist borders. I dream of a homeland that is tamed with peace, where people care and love for each other, without shame nor fear.
