I and myself

July 12, 2026

It's a known cliché to say "this book changed my life". The Inner Game of Tennis changed my perspective on my life and made me reflect on I and myself.[1]

I who decides & self which does

Timothy Gallwey, the author, tells us to stop trying hard, since when we do, our two selves get into conflict: Self 1 tries to take over and overcorrects for Self 2's underperformance, making another "mistake" and ending up in a downward spiral.

mis-take: missing the take?

Instead, we should quiet Self 1 by making it focus on other aspects: observation through our senses. Letting Self 2 take over and do what it needs to do. We should foster a respectful relationship between our two selves.

It gave me a framework to understand the inner battles and wars that I have been waging against myself. After reading the book, I went to my tennis practice, and there I was able to apply the theory successfully and gain inner confidence. Since then, I have been experimenting: whenever I try to do a task, I let go and see how my body accomplishes it. Sometimes effectively, like a well-tuned machine, but sometimes still quite clumsy; yet instead of getting frustrated, I had sympathy for it. I felt that it is only clumsy because it is afraid of me criticizing it, shouting at it and calling it dumb and useless, something that will never be nearly good enough for any amount of satisfaction, and that I will always be in shock when someone finds me doing something remotely capable.

When I tried to open the entry door of the apartment building and the key fell from my hands, I observed my negative feelings boiling up from my core. For once, I ignored them and let them dissipate. I let my body do what it needs to do, after asking it to pick up the keys and try again to open the door, which it then managed effortlessly.

This interaction may be absolutely trivial, but being able to observe something that was hiding before is always an awesome experience.

When I entered, I thought about previous thoughts I had about myself.

I remembered that I always took up a militaristic mindset when I struggled to achieve something. I would wage war against myself. Pushing myself through clearly uncomfortable situations. Pulling all-nighters while hissing and grumbling like a madman. Trying to jam as much information into my mind. Trying hard to finish the seemingly never-ending tasks. Sleepless, tired and battered. The wars waged on. Oftentimes resulting in my defeat against myself. In between periods of intense firefights, whenever possible, a nervous peace would be held. Sheltering in my place. Delving into meaningless trivia and entertainment. Until a sudden provocation, usually from a third-party, left me with no choice other than a strong response, and I had to leave for war again; oh, anxiety, what a terribly useless feeling you are.

From my simple reflections, I observed that I don't have to wage war to do tasks and obtain satisfaction from doing them. I have to be myself, since I am nothing more than myself, and I am enough for myself. I smiled then said to myself "I will no longer wage war against myself, I call for a permanent truce". With a good feeling rushing down my spine, I went up the stairs to the fourth floor and entered my place.

Although the war is over, by no means are the tensions down. I have no doubt that the healing process will be long and uncomfortable. It also won't pass without the occasional skirmish. Years of battle and countless struggles have left permanent scars and bleeding wounds; there is a long trail until our dawn.

Yet the Ego has a terrible habit of getting in the way of life. So it seems, it won't get out of the way without a fight. But I am realizing that the main lifeline of the ego is my validation of it. Me fighting it gives it the ground it needs to stand on. Hence, simply fighting it, yet another part of myself, is not desirable.

I came to realize that the dissonance I always felt may have been the result of the conflict between the two selves. In a paradoxical way, it seems to me that by separating the two selves in the right way, we achieve the ultimate goal of becoming one without interference.[2]

I have gained the ability to observe the inner game; I now also seem able to see how at peace others are with their inner selves. High performers seem totally at peace, and the difference is most noticeable when people are failing. I have also started to notice that animals don't seem to struggle with the same problem, or at least not the way I do. The harmony of their two selves comes more easily, as if this is the natural way of things.

I noticed this when trying to learn to swim and then observing other animals trying to swim for the first time. I have tried many times throughout my life to learn how to swim, with many different people and in many different waters; so far I have failed. The task feels incredibly difficult, and I simply cannot move my hands and legs at the same time in a way that actually gets me swimming. Yet I see animals that have never been in water seemingly learn, within a few tries, to swim across significant bodies of water. This is further supported by the fact that there are no swimming classes for cats or dogs, nor does there seem to be any knowledge passed from parents to their young.

Then again, how do fish learn to swim?
